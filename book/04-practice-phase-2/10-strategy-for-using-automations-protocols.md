# Strategy for Using Automations (Protocols)

[Book contents](../README.md) | [Part contents](README.md)

Protocols support at least three usage patterns.

**First: run pre-built protocols someone else wrote** - essentially following a pre-laid execution path. Simplest, lowest-friction entry point. With minimal effort, you can read and fire two or three protocols each night before sleep and, over time, get clearly observable, measurable shifts in state, reactions, behavior.

**Second: write your own protocols**, scoped to specific topics and states you're actually dealing with. Once you've worked through the free entry-level protocols, the underlying composition principles become obvious. There's rarely a shortage of source material - just look carefully at what's actually interfering with your work, relationships, or general forward motion. Practice builds an intuitive feel fast for how to phrase an instruction and what to weight.

**Third: use automated tooling to simplify work on your own material** - personal problems and states specifically. Simplest example: an auto-processor that ingests a list. Anyone who's used BSFF or similar methods knows how exhausting manually processing a long pre-written list of aspects gets. Here, that execution step - the actual processing - can get offloaded to automation entirely. Free tool for exactly this is on the site (called Auto-Mary).

In practice, these three approaches combine, and generally should. Run pre-built protocols, write your own targeting specific material, and lean on automation wherever it cuts overhead. This combined approach is usually the most effective config overall. In my own experience there were stretches where I actively ran all three in parallel, and other stretches - especially later on - where I worked entirely manually, no automation at all. That happened when new material was scarce, and the overhead of loading aspects into an automated pipeline stopped being worth it. Other times, the reverse: I wrote a dedicated protocol for a specific topic when the problem's structure was already clear, but manually decomposing it into individual elements would've been too tedious to bother with.

Knowing which mode fits a given moment only comes from experience. The only genuinely useful advice here is: **run the work.** Trying to pre-derive "the correct sequence" for every step before starting has diminishing returns. Most questions and doubts resolve themselves once you're actually running the pipeline. If you've decided to do this - start, and keep running it. **People who execute get results.** People who endlessly theorize about the optimal architecture usually stay exactly where they started.

On the automation side specifically - watch throughput. Don't overload the queue. For most people, **2–3 protocols a day** is a reasonably safe default. I wouldn't push past that - risk of a pronounced snapback state rises. Important to understand: overload doesn't always show up immediately. You can fire a protocol and see zero unpleasant effect that day, or even the next. That creates a false read that throughput can scale indefinitely with no cost. Then, a few days later, a sharp backlash can hit - genuinely destabilizing, and a common reason people stop working with Turbo-Suslik entirely.

Which is why the operating principle here is closer to: **"slow is smooth, smooth is fast - as long as it doesn't stop."** A steady, moderate pace puts less strain on the system and produces more stable, deeper results than trying to force throughput past what the pipeline can actually absorb.
