# Working with Problems

[Book contents](../README.md) | [Part contents](README.md)

Over time you'll start noticing something: a significant share of current problems - even "past" ones - turn out to be largely self-constructed by the mind. Not a conclusion you arrive at through reasoning, though. You get there through actual execution - clearing problems one at a time, while incidentally discovering your own architecture from some genuinely unexpected angles.

**There are no unsolvable internal problems.** Any problem either collapses, transforms, or loses relevance - provided it's actually run through the pipeline. Convergence speed varies: some resolve fast, some slow, some need multiple passes. But there's no fundamental exception class here: if a problem exists as a stable mental object, it has a defined structure - and anything with structure can be decomposed and torn down.

Don't expect the end state to be constant euphoria or nonstop happiness, though. More often the baseline shifts toward neutral, even, calm. Sometimes mildly sad, sometimes genuinely pleasant. But one output nearly everyone reports without exception: intrusive mental noise, endless rumination, and dramatization drop sharply. The mind stops running its background job of generating problems out of nothing.

Decomposition as the Basic Working Principle

If all practical work here reduced to one word, it'd be **decomposition.**

Any "big problem" reads as unsolvable exactly as long as it exists as one solid, charged monolith. The actual job is breaking that monolith into its component parts and processing them separately - the same microservices split covered earlier, applied at problem-solving scale rather than single-reaction scale. This is where TS automation saves an enormous amount of time and manual effort.

Take a hypothetical case: `"I'm sick of my job."`

First pass: process it as a whole, for initial charge reduction. Then attention naturally starts latching onto specific subcomponents:

- coworkers are irritating;
- the boss triggers anger or fear;
- hard to get up in the morning;
- a sense of meaninglessness, lack of fulfillment;
- a general "stuck" feeling;
- and so on.

The actual bottleneck lives in a different place for different people. For some it's conflict with authority; for others, an unmet need; for others, old associative transfer from an unrelated pattern. The material itself drives the trace - attention naturally routes to wherever the charge is highest, same as a profiler flagging the hottest function in the call stack.

Result: what looked like one monolithic, chronic problem gradually resolves into a set of discrete, independently addressable components. And it turns out:

- someone's stuck because of an unresolved personal conflict;
- someone's just in the wrong job entirely;
- for someone, the boss is triggering an old school or family pattern;
- for someone, it's genuinely just a boundary or working-conditions fix.

Once the charge discharges, the actual fix is frequently obvious and doesn't require heroic effort. The problem stops being a problem and just becomes a situation - a config change, not an incident.

Basic Work Scheme

Simplified, the pipeline is:

1. **Decompose** - break the problem into its emotions, thoughts, associations, component elements.
1. **Queue** - pass those elements into processing, manually or through automated protocols.
1. **Execute** - fire processing, most often before sleep.

If the problem's deep and multi-layered, the cycle can repeat several times - normal, not a failure state. The process runs automated, and as tension drops, understanding shows up on its own - no forced insight required, no manual root-cause essay needed.

**Important: you don't need to fully analyze every problem to understand its origin, meaning, or "lesson."** That's excess overhead the mind doesn't actually need to run. It's enough to turn one large charged construct into a set of discrete elements and hand them off to processing. The system handles the rest.

The ability to automate this decomposition-and-processing pipeline is, in my view, the actual differentiator of TS. That's what separates it from most approaches that rely on endless manual analysis and digging.

The free section of the site (link at the end of the book) has several hundred protocols available - essentially pre-built decompositions of common human problems, ready to run as-is. This is one of the strongest points of the Turbo-Suslik system: you can just download and run what others have already compiled, instead of digging through problems yourself from scratch. This doesn't just save time - it may open your eyes to things you'd otherwise overlook in your own work. **Leverage this.**

To be precise about what that library actually is: it's not just Turbo-Suslik's own output. A large share of it comes from practitioners themselves, who took existing published psychology books - material never written with this system in mind - and compiled it into working protocols using the same decomposition principle covered above. Someone reads a dense, analytical book on a specific problem, extracts its actual structural insight, and ships it as a runnable protocol other practitioners can just invoke directly, without reading the book itself.

This isn't a fixed body of work handed down by one author - it's a **community-compiled library**, where the barrier to contributing isn't "invent a new technique," it's "find good source material and decompose it correctly." Most psychological knowledge stays locked inside whichever book or therapist's head it originated in, non-transferable except through years of study. Here, once someone does that translation work once, it's a reusable artifact - permanently available to anyone who runs it.

**What Happens Next**

At a certain point, you may not need dedicated processors at all anymore. Just pointing attention at a problem or state is enough - it starts losing density and falling apart on its own. Past that point, the word "problem" itself starts losing relevance as a category.

Exact timing here is individual. Depends heavily on how strongly a given personality tends to grip onto beliefs and constructs - covered in the next chapter.
