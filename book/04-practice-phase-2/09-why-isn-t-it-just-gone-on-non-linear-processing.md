# Why Isn't It Just Gone? (On Non-Linear Processing)

[Book contents](../README.md) | [Part contents](README.md)

A lot of people early on ask a similar question. Something like: *"I ran a protocol targeting need for approval, and it still shows up sometimes - what's going on?"* Or: *"The protocol claims the subconscious will process all fear, or all disbelief, but I still regularly catch myself in fear or doubt states. Does that mean the system's broken?"*

These questions come from a simplified, binary mental model of the psyche - `problem: true/false`, one flag, flip it once, done forever. As if you could run a single `UPDATE` statement, set the value to `false`, and that state never gets read again anywhere in the system. Reality doesn't work like that. The psyche isn't a single boolean. It's a **distributed system**: multi-layered, highly interconnected, with a large number of cross-references, running continuously across many nodes at once.

When you "remove" one element of this structure, you're clearing exactly one active instance - not every cached copy of that data sitting on every node in the cluster. It's entirely possible related material is still sitting on other nodes, replicated, and will resurface later - same shape, or a slightly modified variant. That's exactly why real, stable change needs full processing across the cluster, not spot-fixes on individual nodes. "We cleared it here, didn't touch it there" is close to always ineffective long-term - you've cleared one replica while three others still hold the stale value.

This is the actual reasoning behind protocol strategy. **What matters isn't the one-shot effect of a single run - it's the cumulative effect of many processing cycles converging over time.** And automated processing doesn't necessarily clear everything on its own. In some cases, part of the material needs a manual pass: decomposing the problem and processing the components directly. Remember decomposition here - complex states are rarely a single atomic object; more often they're a bundle of heterogeneous components. That said, "manual" doesn't mean tediously handling every item one by one. You can perfectly well batch the whole set into one array and hand it to an automated processor.

**The strength of automation is clearing a massive volume of minor, background material that would otherwise take years of manual effort.** That's its core value. But don't treat it as a universal solve-everything tool. Automation is powerful and genuinely convenient - it doesn't eliminate the need for some level of user involvement. Some people run almost no manual processing at all and still see solid results - that's more of an individual trait than a reliable baseline expectation.

In terms of raw coverage: depending on topic, automated protocols clear roughly **50 to 80 percent** of the material. The remainder usually needs additional dedicated attention. And even past that, some problems can keep resurfacing for a long time - especially early, deeply rooted patterns formed in childhood, or anything tied to core personality structure. Think of these as values baked into the firmware layer at manufacture time, not something a normal software update touches directly. Themes at this depth rarely clear fast. They weaken and dissolve gradually, as a side effect of processing a lot of other, seemingly unrelated material around them - a direct consequence of the psyche's layered, interconnected architecture, not a special exception.

So a phrase like "the subconscious will process all fear" shouldn't be read literally as a single completed transaction. Fear and doubt can keep surfacing for a long time, across different situations and forms. Not a sign the system's broken - a reflection that attention is still holding onto specific instances and isn't ready to fully release them yet. In these cases, the fix is the same as always: decomposition, decomposition, and decomposition again.

**Address disbelief and doubt directly.** This is one of the most persistent, most widely deployed defaults the mind runs. It gets set early, actively reinforced by the environment for years, and needs sustained, consistent processing to shift. During practice, "swings" - the rollback pattern covered earlier - show up often here specifically: clear visible progress one week, then a few days later, doubt sets in - does this actually work, is any of this trustworthy, does this process even end. Important to understand: the sheer volume of disbelief running in most people's systems is enormous, and few defaults match it for density and persistence. Not a unique failure, not a personal flaw - a typical baseline configuration. And it processes the exact same way as everything else. It just needs more iterations to fully clear.

So, to fix the core point again: the strength of this system isn't a promise of instant output. It's the cumulative effect of consistent, repeated execution. Don't expect a radical internal shift after a couple of runs. Yes, relative to most other approaches, change here happens fast - but it's still a real process that takes time and sustained involvement. A few months of consistent practice is the normal, realistic horizon for serious change.

Last point: newer protocol versions do tend to run deeper and more effectively than earlier ones. Even the most advanced current build, though, doesn't change one basic constraint: a lot of psychological habits were compiled and reinforced over years, sometimes decades. Expecting all of that to clear in a couple of runs is naive. If the intent to change is genuine, you'll see first results reasonably fast. **But stable, deep change still requires systematic, sustained work - no version bump skips that requirement.**
