# Patching vs Refactoring

[Book contents](../README.md) | [Part contents](README.md)

Over years of practice I've watched dozens of different approaches go by. Some work short-term, some only produce an effect as long as you keep the process running and feeding it input, and some actually change the structure of your reactions at the source. Let me break down the difference as plainly as I can.

Broadly, every approach to working with the subconscious reduces to one of two categories - **patching** and **refactoring**. What follows is my personal classification.

Patching is an attempt to change behavior by installing a new pattern from the outside, at runtime, without touching the underlying code. This covers affirmations, suggestion, hypnosis, subliminal "25th frame" tricks (popular a while back), various self-programming methods, and more. The logic is simple: inject a new behavioral routine and hope it overrides the old one on the next call.

The problem: **patching doesn't remove root causes**. It's a hotfix layered on top of a bug you never actually fixed. Looks stable for a while - right up until the process restarts and pulls the original hardcoded value back from source, because that's still what's compiled in.

This is exactly why affirmations tend to fail long-term. As long as you keep re-injecting the override every single session, you might see an effect. Stop injecting it, and the old routine - backed by years of production traffic, reinforced by real experience - comes right back online. The bug was never removed. It was suppressed for the duration of the patch, sometimes at the cost of a nasty merge conflict running in the background the whole time.

Think about where those original values got **hardcoded** in the first place. They weren't randomly generated. They were written, tested, and reinforced over years - sometimes decades - starting in early childhood. The psyche doesn't retain garbage values for no reason; it retains what kept surviving code review under real load.

Then someone latches onto a reasonable premise - "my internal state drives a lot of my outcomes" - gets motivated, and immediately gets sold a "solution": a free course, a guided audio, a stack of phrases to repeat for two hours a day, and supposedly the whole system updates.

That's where the substitution happens. The premise can be entirely correct. The proposed fix is usually a sleight of hand - instead of doing the actual work, you're handed a config override and told it's a rewrite.

How exactly is that supposed to hold up? You're trying to replace a mature, deeply nested, heavily tested legacy codebase - years of commits - with a handful of new lines pasted in at runtime. Two outcomes are typical: the override's TTL expires after a month or two once the motivation runs dry and everything reverts, or you get a standing merge conflict, old logic and new logic both trying to execute on the same trigger, burning cycles on the collision indefinitely.

Then there's the other category - **refactoring**. Actual work on the source, not a wrapper around the symptom and not "learning to live with the exception." You reproduce the bug, trace it to its root commit, and remove it. Once it's actually fixed, it stops throwing - permanently, not until the next restart.

Fixing a problem isn't about force-pushing through it or worshipping raw willpower. "I just need to be stronger than this" is itself just a cruder form of patching - brute-forcing a retry loop against an error you never diagnosed, which is really self-inflicted denial-of-service.

Fixing a problem also isn't endless staring at the same stack trace, admiring how interesting the crash is, or running elaborate intellectual analysis that never ships a patch. It's a concrete process: reproduce it, isolate the root cause, identify the key dependencies, remove them.

BSFF, EFT, and similar techniques are examples of the refactoring category - approaches where you deliberately reduce reactivity and dig out root causes instead of shipping a nicer-looking wrapper around them. Anything working with root causes and raw internal material, not a polished facade, qualifies. Once the legacy junk is actually removed, you can build new patterns on clean ground. Until then, it's old logic versus new logic fighting for the same execution slot - and the old logic usually wins, because it has years of production data backing it.

I'd also classify hypnosis closer to patching than refactoring. In narrow cases it might be a useful auxiliary tool. But I stand for transparency, working from source, staying sober and clear-eyed about what's actually being changed. Hypnosis doesn't offer that: it's installing a closed-source patch without reviewing the diff, and you often have no idea what got modified or on what basis.

Bottom line: patching doesn't interest me, though I'll grant it's occasionally useful as a stopgap for some people in some situations. **What interests me is freedom, and freedom doesn't happen without actually clearing the junk out of the codebase. Only a real fix at the root produces results that don't quietly revert six months later. Everything in this book, by my own classification, belongs to the refactoring category. We're emptying the trash, not repainting it and calling it clean.**
