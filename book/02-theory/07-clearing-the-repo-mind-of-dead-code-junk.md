# Clearing the Repo (Mind) of Dead Code (Junk)

[Book contents](../README.md) | [Part contents](README.md)

An average person who has never seriously audited their own mind has zero idea how much is actually sitting in the repo. The core issue: as a species, we're never onboarded on our own runtime. We get trained on reading, writing, driving, earning money - external APIs, all of them - but nobody teaches attention management, reaction handling, fear, internal conflict resolution, or automatic pattern control. Result: from day one of life, everyone's shipping technical debt straight into their own future build.

By adolescence, most people are already carrying a full package of auto-generated modules that assembled themselves without a spec: complexes, grudges, shame, fears, aversions, internal permission denials, other people's beliefs, standing expectations nobody wrote down. And when someone finally runs a serious audit of their own subconscious material, stuff starts surfacing that makes them grab their head: *"where did all this even come from?"*

The truth is plain and unglamorous: the average install accumulates a massive amount of dead code and orphaned processes. This becomes obvious the second anyone runs real deep-dive work - including professional therapy. It's all in there.

There's aggression toward people and the world, sometimes hidden even from the person running it. There's self-rejection, rejection of reality, a standing sense of being defective by design. There are complexes, irrational assumptions - the overwhelming majority of which aren't even original code. They were cloned, unreviewed, from parent repos: parents, environment, school, culture, media, books, random authority figures.

There are whole layers of doubt, disbelief, cynicism, standing resistance. Massive amounts of resentment, unforgiven grudges, judgment, anger, irritation. Millions of auto-applied tags slapped on everything entering the field of attention: `dangerous`, `ugly`, `beautiful`, `idiot`, `cheap`, `arrogant` - an endless stream of classification output.

And a category of its own - fear. Fear of everything, from death to life itself, with entire rows of fear stacked in between: fear of criticism, loneliness, losing love, judgment, shame, losing face, failure, success, intimacy, responsibility. Hundreds of variants, layered on top of each other like nested exception handlers wrapping every possible input.

Enormous volumes of pain and disappointment, plus decisions made once and never consciously remembered, are still executing today - because nobody ever unset the flag. These stale decisions feed thousands of hardcoded return values: from `can_earn_money = false` to `worth = 0` and `lovable = false`.

What kind of throughput can anyone actually have while running on a system this loaded with conflicts, fears, and self-imposed constraints? And yet almost everyone considers themselves "normal." Pull that config open, though, and "normal" turns out to mean: anxiety, shame, standing internal pressure, chronic tension, suppressed anger, exhaustion, and a never-ending, mostly pointless uptime effort to keep it all from crashing.

Where exactly would well-adjusted systems come from, when from early childhood everyone gets fed hours a day of unfiltered input - information, emotion, scenarios, evaluations they never chose or reviewed? **Garbage in, garbage out** hasn't been repealed. A child absorbs whatever environment it's deployed into. And the environment is, more often than not, running its own unpatched trauma.

Pick a random person, and in the overwhelming majority of cases you'll find someone full of internal contradictions, fear, resentment, complexes, suppression - even if the external build looks polished, well-dressed, smells nice, smiles wide. Most of these bugs are invisible to the person running them, unflagged, because this is just their baseline "normal."

A population made of internally buggy individuals reproduces buggy individuals. Not a conspiracy, not malicious intent - just inertia. The system self-perpetuates: unreviewed parents fork their patterns straight into their children. Those children grow up, ship the same fork downstream, no diff, no review.

To be fair, awareness has been ticking up recently little by little. More parents understand the value of raising kids in an environment of respect, love, acceptance - code review at the source. More people realize the best inheritance you can hand a kid is a psychologically healthy parent repo that isn't propagating its own broken patterns. There's real movement. At population scale, though, it's still a thin trickle against a genuinely enormous legacy install base.

Not exaggerating, not dramatizing. Anyone who actually starts working on themselves and tracing root causes will see roughly the same repo. If there were no way to actually fix any of it, the whole situation would be legitimately hopeless.

It isn't.

Someone actually ready to do the work can deal with their internal gremlins. And yes - this doesn't have to take decades. Timelines vary per system, but with the right approach, real change starts showing up fairly fast. Not effort-free. Not without rough patches. But without mysticism, without cults, without a guru dependency. Self-directed. Self-run. Individually owned.

As you've probably gathered, there's a real backlog here. No need to worry, though, about accidentally deleting working features while you clean out dead code - that you'll somehow lose your capacity for love, kindness, or enjoying anything. Practice shows the opposite: as the junk clears, the healthy modules don't disappear - they get *more* accessible, because fear, conflict, and standing reactivity stop hogging the thread.

In this system, clearing this backlog is a core mechanism - and, importantly, it's actually achievable.

As the repo gets progressively cleaned up, you start understanding how to actually run your own psyche instead of fighting it constantly. To the point where, eventually, it becomes obvious: a significant chunk of your knots and problems was never "you" - just auto-loaded modules from an early build, still running unmaintained, with nobody ever reviewing whether they should still be in production.

That's the point where something shows up that people tend to call "freedom": when the internal system finally idles quietly - not because of a suggestion telling it to, but because the backlog actually got processed.
