# Instructions for the Subconscious

[Book contents](../README.md) | [Part contents](README.md)

Using so-called **instructions** for the subconscious is the core operating principle of the entire system.

The subconscious stores and maintains a massive amount of data about your life: memories, emotional reactions, somatic markers, habitual interpretations, decisions, permission denials, standing expectations, behavioral patterns. The practical validity of this model has already been confirmed by tens of thousands of TS practitioners: the moment someone starts systematic work, connections and root causes start surfacing that the conscious mind - the **Frontend** - failed to notice or couldn't correlate for years.

The subconscious also, as a rule, has direct access to the actual structure of your problems: why a given reaction fires, where the trigger conditions live, which beliefs are keeping a state alive, which decisions got committed at some point and are still executing today. Not claiming it's some kind of "mystical oracle" or omniscient process. But as a practical tool, its memory footprint and processing throughput vastly outclass the conscious mind, which in its default mode mostly runs commentary, self-justification, and rumination loops.

And if a system like that genuinely exists inside you, it would be a waste of available compute not to point it at your actual problems.

Personally, I think we're fortunate here: the subconscious is usable as an internal processor. With that as a tool, you can resolve a huge range of personal issues - not through endless reasoning, but through actual, executed processing of material.

What Is an "Instruction"

In this **working model**, the subconscious is a high-throughput computational system and executor of internal processes. As a metaphor: a personal genie that runs your internal programs. That's a simplification only - no magic involved, just a mechanism of the psyche.

**Technically, an instruction is a function definition.** It has three required parts, same as any function signature: what counts as valid **input** (the material), the **procedure** to run on it (what gets done, and by what rule), and what the **output** should look like (the expected end state). Nothing else qualifies as an instruction - a vague wish or an unstructured intention isn't a function, it's an unhandled request with no signature, and the runtime has nothing to execute.

The whole concept rests on one simple premise: if the subconscious does the bulk of the actual processing work, then a task can be formalized and handed a defined algorithm. In practice: state clearly and specifically what should happen to the material, then trigger execution, then observe the diff.

This is the same principle BSFF runs on: you give the subconscious a clearly defined instruction for what to do with the emotional root of a problem. After that, the person just needs to locate the relevant material, invoke the process with a preset trigger, and the subconscious starts executing.

How It Works in TS

TS runs on the same base principle.

First, you give the subconscious an instruction defining what it should do once you flag a problem and fire execution with a specific phrase - in older terminology, a "magic word," though functionally it's just an **invocation call**.

The mechanism is straightforward: read the instruction once, carefully, out loud or silently. That's the registration step - after that, the subconscious compiles it and holds it ready as a callable procedure.

Some people worry they won't remember the instruction, will forget it, or will "read it wrong." This is almost entirely noise. The subconscious doesn't run on strict rote-memorization matching, character for character. It's not a low-level parser demanding an exact token match - it's closer to a semantic interpreter: what it needs registered is the intent and structure of the procedure - what counts as valid input, what operation runs on it, under what rule, toward what output. Paraphrasing the wording doesn't break the contract, as long as the semantics land intact.

If you want, reread the instruction two or three times - not because the subconscious needs it to compile, but because it settles your own nerves. In most cases, though, one clean read is enough to register the procedure and make it callable.

Processing Modules

Once the instruction's registered, point attention at the material and fire the invocation - the subconscious starts executing.

At this layer the system resembles BSFF. The divergence starts past this point: TS runs multiple distinct processor types, each scoped to a specific job.

For example:

- one instruction handles polarity processing only (neutralization, merge resolution);
- another handles beliefs;
- another handles states;
- another handles relationships, reaction patterns, past episodes, and so on.

Each module is its own function definition: a defined input type, a defined procedure, a defined output condition. No shared, one-size-fits-all handler - scoped modules, each doing one job properly.

Macros: The Most Powerful Part

A number of TS instructions are structured as literal **macros**: a single instruction expands into and runs a whole sequence of lower-level calls - batch processing, in the direct sense of the term.

This is the single most powerful piece of the system, because it lets you hand the subconscious a complex, multi-step job - not scoped to one episode or one emotion, but to entire layers of material at once.

For example, you can assign a batch job covering:

- every instance in your life where a specific negative state occurred;
- every belief and decision sustaining that state;
- every downstream consequence that got locked in by that experience;
- every related past episode;
- and, where relevant, other people's stance toward this material and toward you (see the chapter on Circular Processing), including forgiveness and release.

All of it runs automatically, in the background, sometimes overnight. In practice, you're using the subconscious as a compute cluster: not "sit and think it through," but "assign the job, let it run."

Why This Is Needed

This makes it possible to process large volumes of material relatively fast and cheap. What used to take weeks or months of manual digging can now run as a single batch job against a large body of material.

Naturally, there are limits and nuances. Manual, targeted work on a single specific issue can sometimes produce a denser, higher-resolution result than a generalized batch pass. But the gain in speed, volume, and overall simplicity that automation buys you is significant enough that, for me, the tradeoff is obvious.

Anything a batch pass doesn't fully clear can always get refined manually afterward, or folded into a separate macro and run before sleep.

Technical Principles of the System

The system in this book runs on three core principles:

- **Processing modules** - subconscious instructions, each defining an algorithm scoped to a specific type of material.
- **Macros** - composite procedures that batch-process large volumes of material by chaining modules together.
- **Automation** - offloading execution to the subconscious runtime for speed and simplicity.

The tradeoff for that convenience and scale: results are often not immediate. This isn't a strongly-consistent, synchronous system - it's closer to **eventual consistency**: the write lands now, but the state doesn't always converge on the observable output right away. Sometimes it takes days, sometimes weeks.

I don't treat this as a defect. The rate of change is more than sufficient. It's just that in this system, most of the real work doesn't come from an immediate "wow" spike - it comes from actual internal processing that lands and sticks.
