# The Operating Principle of the "Process this" Processor

[Book contents](../README.md) | [Part contents](README.md)

``<br>
`Process this` isn't a standalone technique - it's a **control macro**. One short call kicks off several internal processes at once, all targeting the selected mental material. "Mental material" here means anything attention can hold: thoughts, emotions, beliefs, reactions, memories, evaluations, and the automatic patterns wired to them.

Inside the macro, several independent submodules get invoked. Some are ported and adapted from processes used in other systems (Živorad Slavinski's work, in particular - prior art we build directly on), others are proprietary, synthesized across multiple sources. This chapter isn't trying to prove any one model correct. Goal is just giving a working mental model of what actually fires under the hood, so using the processor later doesn't leave you wondering "wait, what's actually happening in there."

For a concrete case, take a hypothetical problem: `"I hate Joe."` Attention flags this material (via the phrasing itself), `Process this` fires, and a sequence of automatic subroutines kicks off.

**Step 1 - trace the stack.** The system checks past experience and traces back through the history for episodes that may have originated or amplified this reaction - root commits, in the earlier terminology. Those episodes get processed to reduce or eliminate their emotional charge, via separate dedicated subroutines.

**Step 2 - decompose the monolith.** Next it attempts to break the original problem into smaller components. Most persistent reactions aren't monolithic - they're a composite of specific triggers, evaluations, and interpretations bundled together. In this example: `"his tone irritates me,"` `"he reminds me of someone else,"` `"I don't accept his social circle,"` and so on. Automatically decomposing a monolith into its constituent parts lets each piece get processed faster and with more precision - basically a microservices split applied to a single tangled reaction. Even if this decomposition never surfaces to conscious awareness, the system attempts it in the background regardless.

**Step 3 - flush stale config.** This block targets previously made decisions, conclusions, and beliefs tied to the material - including values set years ago that are no longer referenced in any conscious documentation but are still linked at runtime. Childhood generalizations, protective decisions, unconscious behavioral rules. Say, at some point a value like `people_like_that = untrustworthy` got hardcoded - long forgotten consciously, still being read by the current reaction every single time. These stale values, plus whatever justification and evaluation got compiled alongside them, get processed here too.

**Step 4 - process the beliefs layer.** In parallel, beliefs and convictions attached to the problem itself, its components, and the people or objects in play get processed - including beliefs about whether change is even possible, about the "nature" of the problem, about your own available resources. This layer targets the rigid cognitive structures actually load-bearing the problem, not the surface symptom.

**Step 5 - integrate the subprocesses.** A separate stage targets personality aspects - the functional subpersonalities involved in generating or maintaining the reaction. The processor runs an automated, background-adapted version of aspect work here. These parts aren't suppressed or killed - they're merged into a more coherent overall configuration, same integration mechanism covered in the defragmentation chapter. In practice this shows up as a gradual drop in internal contradiction and conflict.

**Step 6 - clear the emotional queue.** Next, the system processes emotional charge tied to resentment, non-acceptance, judgment, and grievance - toward others and toward yourself - plus related evaluations of the world, reality, and events generally. Purpose here: drain the emotional charge actually fueling the problem.

**Step 7 - integration pass.** After that, the material gets processed as a single unit, not as separate parts - a merge commit consolidating every incremental patch from the previous steps into one coherent state. Several subprocesses run here specifically to stabilize the result.

**Step 8 - commit and close.** Final stage is auxiliary. Once the problematic material's been eliminated or weakened, the system runs a symbolic "closure" step - writing a neutral internal representation that blocks the old configuration from auto-reverting. Doesn't matter exactly how you interpret this mechanically - imagined operation, cognitive marker, a completion flag, whatever model you like. What matters: in practice, this step measurably increases result stability, same reasoning as closing a database transaction properly instead of leaving it open - an uncommitted change sits more exposed to rollback. Theory's secondary here; the observed effect is what counts.

So: `Process this` doesn't trigger one action. It kicks off a full pipeline that dismantles the problem from multiple angles sequentially. Some pieces - handling resistance, or fear of the problem returning, for instance - get pulled out of this macro entirely and run in separate automated protocols instead. That separation's deliberate: it keeps manual work simpler and pushes overall system throughput up.

Bottom line: this isn't a "magic command." It's a standardized way to trigger internal processing on material without manually supervising every single stage - which is exactly what makes the processor general-purpose, usable against almost any kind of mental content.
