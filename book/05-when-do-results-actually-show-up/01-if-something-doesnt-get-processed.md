# If Something Doesn’t Get Processed

[Book contents](../README.md) | [Part contents](README.md)

What do you actually do when a specific problem stubbornly refuses to clear? As a rule, it means you've hit a topic whose root structure runs deeper and more branched than it initially appeared. Classic case: **self-dislike.** This is a large, multi-layered structure, assembled from early childhood out of dozens - sometimes hundreds - of smaller sub-elements: `"I don't deserve anything good,"` `"I don't deserve a promotion,"` `"I don't deserve a normal relationship,"` `"I don't deserve..."` - and each of those is itself its own bundle of interconnected sub-parts. Expecting a single instruction like `"eliminate all self-dislike"` to produce a fast, deep resolution is naive. Not because the tool's broken - because the actual problem graph is far more complex than the surface query suggested.

Part of this expectation gets fed by the same misconception covered in the root-commit chapter: belief in a single root cause. Won't re-litigate it here, just flagging the downstream consequence: belief in a single root actively interferes with real work. Someone tries to hit the problem head-on instead of decomposing it and dismantling the structure piece by piece. No fast result shows up, so they go hunting for "more powerful" techniques that promise to clear everything in one shot. Standard outcome: wasted time, wasted effort, disappointment, and the whole practice gets abandoned.

At some point, the bottleneck isn't the method - it's the physical constraints of the actual hardware. Working deep topics means restructuring significant parts of the psyche, which means real physiological change at the nervous-system level. Old, stable connections between neurons weaken and break down; new ones form in their place. These are physically real processes, measurable with objective methods in a proper lab. No matter how flexible your thinking is, the underlying hardware simply won't let a job like this get swallowed in one pass. Which is exactly why fanaticism is the wrong move here. Overload just produces exhaustion, not faster convergence. What actually works: patience and consistent decomposition.

The physiological layer, by the way, is one of the reasons pendulums exists. As the nervous system restructures, it burns a genuinely significant amount of resource, which produces a specific kind of fatigue a lot of people have never encountered before. Any neuropsychologist will confirm habits, beliefs, even stable concepts have a concrete neural substrate. Put plainly: a habit is a group of neurons wired into a relatively stable network. That network fires as a single unit, instantly, which is exactly what makes habitual reactions so energy-efficient. Which is why the brain defaults to the well-worn path - same reason a cached lookup beats a fresh computation every time.

From early childhood, a person gets trained to fire specific states: guilt, shame, a sense of being bad or unworthy, an expectation of suffering, beliefs about the world's basic unfairness, and so on. Over years and decades, these patterns get fully automated. Sometimes one thought or one external trigger is enough to fire the entire network at once. During processing, structures like this get gradually discharged and dismantled - which is exactly what can produce setbacks: the same rollback pattern covered earlier.

So if something doesn't clear immediately, that's not a reason to quit. It's a signal the problem's structure is more complex than it first looked. Trace its components, inspect it from multiple angles, get better at analyzing and decomposing the material into aspects. Work carefully, without forcing throughput. Childhood, relationships with parents, patterns internalized from them and from the environment, beliefs, "life lessons" - all of it needs especially thorough, granular processing. Yes, this can run long and isn't always pleasant. But if the goal is an actual result, there's no way around it.

In my own experience, the overwhelming majority of problems do eventually resolve - some quite fast. More persistent themes, tied to general patterns of attention and thinking, gradually shift into the category of **controlled states.** They might still fire occasionally, but the person clearly sees the source and the mechanism, and either consciously intercepts it or just observes it calmly. These stop poisoning daily life or generating self-flagellation. And with continued work, they keep weakening further, even without dedicated targeting.

So if you hit a state that reads as "unworkable," no need to drop the practice over it. Sometimes the better move is temporarily setting that specific topic aside and continuing the general plan. Over time, you either come back to it with deeper visibility into its structure, or find it's already noticeably weaker from indirect processing elsewhere in the system.

Bottom line's simple. **Stubborn, difficult topics showing up is normal and expected.** Not a signal to panic, not a sign the tool's broken. Just keep running the work. In most cases, after enough time passes, you'll either smile remembering a problem that used to exist at all - or you won't even be able to recall clearly what it was actually about.
