# When Do Results Actually Show Up?

[Book contents](../README.md)

As already covered in "Is It Worth Trying?", first changes with normal practice usually become noticeable at the two-to-three-week mark. Context: over 18-plus years of this system running in production, a huge range of people in very different states have gone through it. Some later admitted, honestly, that up through month three they were essentially operating blind - no sense of progress, no ability to assess anything cleanly, sometimes barely any desire to keep going, because their starting state was that heavy. Timeline estimates mostly exist for people evaluating whether to invest time in an unfamiliar tool at all. They don't directly affect the actual process or its outcome - same as a rough ETA doesn't change what the job actually does once it's running.

For anything close to serious, stable results, two system-specific facts matter. First: results here run heavily delayed. Second: with daily, intensive work early in practice, overall state often makes it genuinely hard to assess what's actually changing in real time - you're inside the noise, not observing it from outside.

The delay is the direct cost of getting automated background processing of large volumes of material at all. Completely normal for a specific problem to not clear immediately, but weeks or months later - the person just keeps running the practice and then notices, almost incidentally, that something that used to trigger regularly just... doesn't anymore. Some things clear fast, some take longer, some persist and need repeated processing from multiple angles - sometimes ones that don't look obviously connected to the original issue at all.

One of the core conditions for this system working: **don't wait for results, just run the work.** Results either show up over time, or it becomes clearer what still needs processing before they can. People who constantly hunt for confirmation tend to end up disappointed - instead of building cumulative throughput, they burn attention on polling and self-checking. Classic anti-pattern. Far more effective to process and move forward. The effect surfaces later, or the next useful direction becomes clearer either way. This system is poorly suited to solving one or two isolated problems in isolation - **it shows its actual value through total, sustained work across all available material.** "Process everything reachable and move on without expecting a signal" produces the strongest long-term change.

On overall state during regular processing: the initial window - roughly the first two to three months - often reads like sustained high CPU load: elevated fatigue, instability, periodic rollback states. During this window, internal material can surface in a dense, near-continuous stream, creating a felt sense that it never ends. Against that backdrop, spotting positive shifts is genuinely hard, especially if you're inside a rollback where the internal monologue collapses to "nothing's changing, this is pointless." Trying to objectively evaluate results from inside that state is close to meaningless.

Over time, load intensity drops, gaps start appearing, things get subjectively easier, and changes become visible and stable. Not a sudden step function - a gradual decline as the queue drains.

So in practice, the task is simple: for the first two to three months, work steadily and consistently, with zero expectation of immediate output. Some intermediate shifts may surface earlier, but given how unstable overall state is during this window, don't draw hard conclusions from them yet.

Fixing the core point one more time: don't chase an instant effect, and don't sit around waiting for one either. Work for the cumulative result, process everything reachable, and that approach - over time - is what produces genuinely serious, sometimes unexpected change.
