# What Does Turbo-Suslik Actually Do?

## A Cybernetic Model of the Protocol as an Instruction for the Brain

The simplest practical answer to the question “How does Turbo-Suslik work?” is: take some material, run a protocol, and see what changes.

For practice, that is often enough. But if we want to understand the mechanism without mysticism, a more interesting question appears: **what can a text-based protocol actually do to the real biological neural network inside the brain?**

The short answer is:

**A protocol does not rewrite the brain directly. It gives the processing system a task: what material to activate, what related elements to search for, what to do with them, and what state to work toward. If this processing produces a stable change in the response to the material, then the functioning of the corresponding neural networks must also have changed in some way.**

In other words, a protocol can be viewed as a prompt. The difference is that the prompt is executed not by an LLM on a GPU, but by the person’s own nervous system.

> **Scope of this model.** What follows is not an experimentally established neural mechanism of Turbo-Suslik. It is an engineering model: a translation of protocol logic into the language of known processes in memory, learning, attention, and neuroplasticity. Establishing the exact mechanism of TS would require direct research on TS itself.

## A Protocol Really Is Similar to a Prompt

The analogy with an LLM is useful here.

When we give a language model a prompt, we do not open its weight matrix and manually change the numbers. We change the context, define the task, and influence the path of computation. The same model with the same weights can produce very different outputs under different prompts because its current activation pattern and processing trajectory change.

The brain differs in one important respect: **it computes and learns at the same time**. Current activation can influence later plasticity. Experience, repetition, emotional significance, prediction error, learning, and sleep can all affect the strength and organization of neural connections.

So the phrase “the protocol changes the weights” is too crude. A more accurate version is:

**the protocol first changes what the system is doing now; if this new mode of processing becomes established, it can change how likely the brain is to follow the old route next time.**

This is consistent with the biology of learning. In a classic 1998 paper, Eric Kandel explicitly linked durable psychological change to changes in brain function and neural connectivity [1]. Studies have also reported changes in neural activity and functional connectivity after psychotherapy [9, 10].

## What “Weights” Mean Here

Artificial neural networks have convenient mathematical weight matrices. The brain is more complicated.

There is no single number like `W(fear of snakes)`. There is a large distributed network involving sensory features, episodic memories, bodily responses, expectations, learned actions, word meanings, attention, and habitual interpretations of what is happening.

So in this article, “weights” should be understood not as a literal matrix but as **the strength and probability of habitual transitions**.

For example:

`snake → danger → bodily mobilization → avoidance`

or:

`criticism → "I am bad" → defense → conflict`

or:

`memory → shame → self-attack → more shame`

If a route has been used thousands of times, the system enters it almost instantly. The stimulus has barely been evaluated, but the conclusion has already been reached, the body has already reacted, and the internal commentary is already running.

These automatic routes are what TS works with.

## “Material” Is Not a Sentence. It Is a Network

Suppose a person says: **“I am afraid of snakes.”**

At the language level, that is one sentence. At the nervous-system level, it may correspond to an entire graph:

- specific episodes;
- images and sounds;
- stories told by parents or other people;
- films and news reports;
- bodily tension;
- expectation of pain or death;
- a habit of avoidance;
- the belief “I will not be able to cope”;
- the need to keep checking the environment;
- a memory of someone being bitten;
- fear of the fear response itself.

This is why TS protocols expand the initial material. The target is not only the sentence, but also episodes, thoughts, emotions, bodily reactions, decisions, beliefs, resistance, expectations, relationships, identities, and the links between them.

Cybernetically, the logic is simple: **if the problem is a network, working only with its label is not enough. You have to move through the connected nodes.**

This is one reason TS differs from trying to replace an old thought with a new one. Saying “snakes are safe” does not necessarily change a network that has spent twenty years learning the opposite. The old route may remain intact and reactivate under a sufficiently strong stimulus.

TS approaches the problem from the other direction: instead of trying to shout over the old network, it attempts to reduce that network’s ability to seize control automatically.

## The Brain Is Constantly Predicting

A useful modern framework for understanding this is **predictive processing** [2, 3].

In a highly simplified form, the brain does not wait passively for the world to tell it what is happening. It constantly generates predictions: what am I about to see, what does it mean, is it dangerous, what will the body feel, what should I do next?

Incoming signals are compared with the model already in place. If that model has spent years saying “snake = immediate threat,” the brain does not hold a philosophical discussion about bite statistics every time a snake appears. It launches the practiced response package.

So a psychological problem often exists not as one “wrong thought,” but as **a stable configuration of predictions, evaluations, and reactions**.

A stimulus produces a prediction. The prediction changes the body and attention. The changed body and attention then make the old prediction feel even more convincing.

For example:

`thought of danger → tension → feeling that "the danger is real" → increased attention to threat`

Such structures can persist for a long time because the reaction itself partly generates evidence for the old model.

## What Happens After a Protocol Is Started

If we remove internal TS terminology and keep only function, several fairly clear operations remain.

### 1. The System Gets an Address

First, we specify **what to work with**.

This may be an episode, a fear, a person, a belief, a bodily response, a recurring thought, or a broad thematic block.

This matters more than it may seem. Instead of the vague “I feel bad,” there is now a defined processing target. Attention and memory have an address around which related material can be activated.

### 2. The Material Becomes Active

To change an old response, the corresponding network has to become active to some degree.

Memory research shows that retrieval is not always like opening a dead file from storage. Under some conditions, reactivated memories can become temporarily plastic and then stabilize again. This class of processes is known as **memory reconsolidation** [4–6].

It is important not to overstate this. Not every retrieved memory automatically enters full reconsolidation, and the process has important boundary conditions. Modern reviews discuss in detail the conditions under which retrieval leads to reconsolidation, extinction, or neither [6].

For the TS model, the broader point is enough: **activating old material creates an opportunity for updating it; material that remains inactive is much harder to change.**

### 3. The Network Unfolds

Once the target is activated, neighboring elements can appear: episodes, phrases, sensations, associations, old decisions, objections, fears of consequences, and resistance to the change itself.

In TS this is not a processing error. It is exactly what we want.

If “I am afraid of snakes” is supported not by one cause but by many connected structures, then durable change requires touching more than the central sentence.

That is why protocols use constructions such as “all related episodes,” “all causes and consequences,” “all resistance,” “all beliefs,” and “all attitudes toward the material.”

You can think of this as graph traversal: the task is not to process the attractive name of the problem, but the network that keeps the problem running.

### 4. The Meaning of the Material Changes


TS does not need to erase information about what happened. The task is different: **stored information should stop automatically controlling the present.**

A person may remember an episode of humiliation from twenty years ago perfectly well, without the memory automatically launching shame, anger, bodily contraction, and three hours of internal argument every time it appears.

In TS language, this is called “removing the charge.”

In more neutral language, we can describe it as reducing the **control significance** of the material: it captures less attention, triggers the threat system less strongly, demands less immediate action, and is less able to pull the system into the old automatic route.

Concepts such as **salience** and **precision** from predictive models are useful here, although they should not be treated as literal translations of the TS term “charge.” They simply provide a way to describe the functional effect: the old signal is no longer treated as so important, reliable, and mandatory.

### 5. The Response Is Updated

If old material becomes active during or after processing, but the usual response no longer unfolds in the same way, the system receives a new experience.

The old prediction receives less confirmation: “this will definitely overwhelm me,” “this is unbearable,” “I must defend myself,” “there is no other way.”

If the change persists and repeats, the old route becomes less accessible and less obligatory.

This overlaps with several known learning mechanisms: reappraisal, memory updating, inhibitory learning, and ordinary neuroplasticity. There is no need to declare any single one of them “the secret mechanism of TS.” Several processes may contribute at the same time.

## What Cognitive Reappraisal Has to Do With It

A well-studied example of meaning changing emotional response is **cognitive reappraisal**.

A person interprets a situation differently, and the emotional response changes. A meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies found consistent involvement of cognitive-control regions during reappraisal, together with modulation of systems involved in emotional responding [7].

TS is not classical reappraisal. The goal is not to consciously invent a nicer story and believe it. But the underlying point matters: **the meaning of a stimulus is not permanently welded to the stimulus itself. If processing and interpretation change, the system’s response can change as well.**

That is a physical process, even though language can trigger it.

## Can Words Really Change the Brain?

The fact that a protocol consists of text sometimes creates a strange intuition: surely a collection of words cannot physically change anything.

It can, because words are not ink on paper from the brain’s point of view.

A sentence that is understood changes current brain activity just as a perceived object, a heard sound, or an instruction does. If words cause a person to recall an event, shift attention, activate an emotion, inhibit a habitual response, or learn a different one, ordinary learning mechanisms take over from there.

Psychotherapy makes this especially obvious. Research on CBT does not tell us anything directly about TS, but it demonstrates an important general point: **psychological work that relies heavily on language, attention, reappraisal, and new experience can be accompanied by measurable changes in brain activity and functional connectivity** [9, 10].

For example, Shou and colleagues reported changes in amygdala connectivity with cognitive-control networks after CBT in patients with depression and PTSD [9]. A systematic review and ALE meta-analysis by Yuan and colleagues identified several consistent task-related activation changes after CBT across psychiatric disorders, while also showing that the findings do not reduce to a single simple “therapy area” in the brain [10].

So the opposition between “just words” and “the real brain” is false.

**Words are one kind of input to a real brain.**

## What Then Happens to the “Weights” After Successful Processing?

If we keep the neural-network analogy, the system may initially prefer routes like these:

| Route | Before processing | After stable processing |
|---|---|---|
| stimulus → threat | nearly automatic transition | threat is evaluated less automatically |
| material → attention | fast fixation and repeated return | attention lets go more easily |
| thought → body | rapid mobilization and tension | bodily response is weaker or absent |
| episode → self-description | “this proves what kind of person I am” | the episode remains part of experience but defines identity less strongly |
| belief → behavior | habitual avoidance or defense | more response options become available |

This does not mean we measured a particular synapse and watched its coefficient fall from `0.83` to `0.41`.

It is a functional description: **the old route becomes less compulsory.**

In functional terms, a stable change appears when something that previously launched a familiar internal sequence stops doing so, or does so much more weakly. There does not have to be a dramatic internal event.

## Why “Indifference” Is Not a Bad Technical Term

TS often uses the idea of **complete indifference** as an end state of processing.

From the outside, this can sound like a philosophy of apathy. Technically, that is not what it means.

Here, indifference means **absence of compulsory capture**.

You can understand that a snake may be dangerous and still avoid picking it up with bare hands. You can remember an unpleasant person and still prefer not to deal with them. You can judge an action as wrong.

But none of this requires automatic rage, fear, shame, a three-hour internal monologue, and a knot in the stomach.

The information remains. The compulsion decreases.

This matches an old TS formulation: **the past changes from an active control program into an archive.**

## Why This Is Deprogramming

Reprogramming tries to install a new answer on top of the old one:

`I am bad → I am good`

`the world is dangerous → the world is wonderful`

`I am afraid → I am fearless`

The problem is that the old automatic pattern is under no obligation to disappear because of a new declaration. You may simply end up with two competing constructions, while the older one has years of actual learning behind it.

Deprogramming asks a different question:

`I am bad → what is this construction made of, and why does it have so much control over my response?`

Then it works with the material that gives the construction its power.

If the old material loses control weight, there is no need to fill the space immediately with a new ideology. The person simply has more freedom of response and can decide what to do in the actual situation.

In this sense, TS does not so much write a new program as **reduce the rigidity of the old one**.

## Where the Cybernetics Is

If we strip away psychological vocabulary completely, the structure becomes almost boring:

**Input:** selected material.

**Control program:** the protocol.

**Working system:** memory, attention, emotional and bodily responses, semantic associations, automatic behavior patterns.

**Feedback:** what appears during and after processing: thoughts, emotions, resistance, related material, changes in response.

**Correction:** additional processing of what remains active.

**Outcome criterion:** the material remains available as information but no longer launches the old automatic package.

That is the cybernetic core of TS:

**control of the system’s own processing through a formalized instruction and feedback loop.**

## Why This Is Not Reiki or “Remote Healing”

The distinction lies in the structure of the process.

In Reiki, remote healing, and similar systems, the central mechanism is usually described as an external influence: a practitioner, healer, or other intermediary supposedly transmits energy or changes another person’s state at a distance.

TS does not require such an intermediary at all.

There is a person, their own material, and an instruction that tells their own system how to work with that material. Nothing has to be “transmitted” from one person to another. No healer is required, no session is required, and no external channel is required.

Once internal terminology is removed, the structure is straightforward:

`mental content → language instruction → directed internal processing → changed response`

That is why TS is best understood not as a ritual, but as a **protocol interface to one’s own cognitive-emotional system**.

## The Shortest Model

If everything above is compressed into a few lines, the model looks like this.

The brain contains stable networks linking events, meanings, emotions, bodily reactions, and behavior. Some of these networks become excessively automatic and continue to control a person years after they were formed.

In this model, a TS protocol uses language to tell the system which cluster to activate and how to process it. The material becomes active, connected elements unfold, their significance changes, and the strength of habitual transitions is reduced. If the change stabilizes, the old route becomes less probable and less compulsory.

**The memory does not have to disappear. What disappears is the necessity to execute the old response every time the memory is activated.**

The most accurate short formulation is therefore:

> **Turbo-Suslik uses language as an interface for controlling the processing of mental material. A protocol directs the system through a connected graph of old reactions and toward updating their significance, so that stored information no longer automatically controls the present.**

Even shorter:

> **TS reduces the control weight of old material.**

In engineering terms: neural network, input data, control instruction, feedback, and plasticity.
